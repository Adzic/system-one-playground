Beat 3 · The mechanism

Start with the arithmetic of the null. Under no relationship, the standard deviation of a sample correlation is

\[\operatorname{sd}(r) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} = 0.01 \qquad (n = 10{,}000)\]

Take the maximum over 200 candidates and the loudest spurious feature lands around \(0.03\), as Part A shows. Spread the true \(R^2\) of \(0.01\) across the real features and a typical genuine effect has a correlation near \(0.007\). The noise, once you search over it, is several times louder than the signal.

Every split a GBDT considers is a small version of Part B. At each node it scans all 200 features and their thresholds and keeps the best in-sample gain. The winner won partly on merit, if any, and partly because its noise pointed the right way, and the algorithm cannot separate the two. That is the winner's curse, run thousands of times per forest. Then boosting makes it structural: the first few trees may catch what little signal exists, and every tree after that is fit to residuals that are essentially pure noise. The training score climbs, the importances look precise, and the honest out-of-sample \(R^2\) sits near zero or below, exactly as in Part B, where the train winner's \(R^2\) routinely collapses to nothing on the test half.

Ridge has the opposite geometry. It never searches and never selects. It shrinks every coefficient smoothly toward zero, spreads weight across correlated features instead of crowning one, and as the penalty grows it degrades monotonically toward predicting the mean, which in this regime is nearly the right answer. Its worst case is a shrug. The GBDT's worst case is a confident, elaborate fiction with a feature importance chart attached.

The working cure is procedural, not heroic. Make ridge, or even the mean, the champion and put the burden of proof on the flexible model. Run a permutation benchmark: shuffle \(y\), run the entire pipeline end to end, and record the validation score the machinery produces from pure noise; if your real score sits inside that null distribution, you do not have a model. Use repeated cross-validation and report the distribution of scores, not a point estimate, because at \(R^2 \approx 0.01\) the differences worth chasing are smaller than the noise in a single split's metric.

Beat 4 · At work

This is the daily shape of signal screening: hundreds of candidate events, behaviors, and codes, each tested for association with an outcome that is mostly noise. A screening loop that ranks candidates by raw lift is Part B with a bigger budget. It will surface a top ten list every single time, including on data where nothing is there, and the list will look like insight. The discipline that separates a screening loop from a noise harvest is the confirmation machinery: a held-out time window the discovery pass never sees, a permutation run to price what the machinery invents for free, and incremental value measured inside the current model's score bands rather than raw association. If your pipeline, run on shuffled \(y\), produces validation scores that look like your real ones, you have nothing, and it is far better to learn that from the shuffle than from a VP.
