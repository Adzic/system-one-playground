PROBLEM 5 · SIMULATION

Build a Correlation

You have two independent standard normals, \(Z_1, Z_2 \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), and nothing else. Write \(X\) and \(Y\) as linear combinations of them so that both \(X\) and \(Y\) are standard normal and \(\operatorname{Corr}(X, Y) = \rho\), for any \(\rho\) you are handed. This is the atom of every Monte Carlo engine ever built.

Beat 3 · The mechanism

Two constraints, two unknowns. Write \(X = Z_1\) and \(Y = a Z_1 + b Z_2\). Any linear combination of independent normals is normal, with mean 0 for free. The covariance constraint sets \(a\): \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(Z_1, a Z_1 + b Z_2) = a \operatorname{Var}(Z_1) = a\), and since both variables will have variance 1, covariance is correlation, so \(a = \rho\). The variance constraint sets \(b\): independence kills the cross term, so

\[\operatorname{Var}(Y) = a^2 + b^2 = \rho^2 + b^2 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b = \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\]

That is the entire construction:

\[X = Z_1, \qquad Y = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\, Z_2\]

Read it as a budget. \(Y\) has one unit of variance to spend. It spends \(\rho^2\) of it on the shock it shares with \(X\), which is what makes them move together, and the remaining \(1 - \rho^2\) on a private shock, which is what keeps \(Y\) from being a mere copy of \(X\). The weights are standard deviations, so they enter squared: that is why \(b\) is the square root of the leftover, not the leftover itself. At \(\rho = 0.8\): spend \(0.64\) shared, \(0.36\) private, and \(b = \sqrt{0.36} = 0.6\). At the extremes the budget degenerates honestly: \(\rho = 1\) spends everything on the shared shock and \(Y = X\); \(\rho = 0\) spends nothing and the two are independent.

In matrix form, \(\begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix} = L \begin{pmatrix} Z_1 \\ Z_2 \end{pmatrix}\) with \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho & \sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \end{pmatrix}\), and \(L\) is exactly the Cholesky factor of the correlation matrix: \(L L^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Nothing about the number 2 matters. For any covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) with Cholesky factor \(L\), the recipe \(X = L Z\) turns independent standard normals into a draw with covariance \(\Sigma\), because \(\operatorname{Cov}(L Z) = L \operatorname{Cov}(Z) L^\top = L L^\top = \Sigma\). Every correlated simulation you will ever run is this page, with a bigger matrix.

Beat 4 · At work

This construction is the load-bearing wall of applied simulation. Correlated defaults in a credit portfolio, correlated returns in a stress test, synthetic data that must reproduce a target correlation structure before anyone trusts conclusions drawn from it, the Gaussian copula that wires marginal distributions to a dependence structure: all of them are \(X = L Z\) underneath. The failure mode is just as concrete. Teams routinely specify the marginals carefully and the correlations casually, then simulate a world whose joint behavior nobody intended, because the dependence was never actually constructed, only gestured at. The discipline this problem teaches is the professional one: a correlation is not a property you declare, it is a mixture of shared and private shocks that you build. If you cannot construct it, you cannot simulate it, and if you cannot simulate it, your stress test is a story.
