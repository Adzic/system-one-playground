The Event You Never See

Estimate \(p = \mathbb{P}(X > 4)\) for \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) by Monte Carlo: draw 10,000 samples, count how many land past 4, divide. A perfectly reasonable plan, which will almost certainly tell you the probability is zero. It is not zero. It is small, and small is a different country.

Beat 3 · The mechanism

The truth is \(p = \mathbb{P}(X > 4) = 3.17 \times 10^{-5}\), about one in thirty thousand. The plain estimator is unbiased, which sounds reassuring and is nearly worthless here: the number of hits in a run is binomial with mean \(Np = 0.317\), so about \(73\%\) of runs see no hit at all and report \(\hat{p} = 0\). Its relative error runs at

\[\frac{\operatorname{SE}(\hat{p})}{p} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{Np}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.317}} \approx 177\%\]

To drag that down to \(10\%\) you need \(Np \approx 100\), which means roughly \(3.2\) million samples. The failure is pure variance: almost the entire budget is spent sampling a region that contributes nothing, waiting for a lottery the budget cannot afford.

Importance sampling changes where the samples live. Draw \(X_i\) from a proposal \(g\), here \(\mathcal{N}(4, 1)\), and reweight by the likelihood ratio of target density \(f\) to proposal:

\[\hat{p}_{\mathrm{IS}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\{X_i > 4\}\, \frac{f(X_i)}{g(X_i)}, \qquad \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = e^{\,8 - 4x}\]

Under the proposal, about half the draws land past 4, so the indicator is no longer rare. And on the event itself the weights are tame: for \(x > 4\), \(e^{8 - 4x} \le e^{-8} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{-4}\), so no single term can explode and hijack the average. The result, as the trace shows, is a few percent of relative error on the same 10,000 draws that bought the plain runner nothing. The ideal proposal is the conditional distribution of \(X\) given the event, the distribution of \(X\) restricted to \((4, \infty)\); you cannot sample from it directly, since knowing it is the whole problem, but shifting the mean to the boundary point of the rare set is the standard cheap approximation, and here it is nearly perfect.

Beat 4 · At work

Every domain has its version of past 4. The default rate on a pristine loan segment. The fraud pattern that occurs a handful of times a year. The component failure a reliability team must bound before launch. The extreme loss a risk committee actually loses sleep over. In each case the event that matters is rare by construction, and in each case plain simulation, and plain historical counting, fail in the same dangerous way: not loudly, with an error, but silently, with a zero. A dashboard that says "we simulated it and it never happened" feels like evidence and is almost none; with an expected hit count below one, the simulation was never capable of seeing the event it was asked about. The professional habit this builds is to ask, before trusting any simulated or counted frequency, what the expected number of occurrences in the sample actually is. If it is near or below one, the estimate is a coin flip dressed as a measurement, and the fix is to sample where the event lives, importance sampling, stratification, conditional methods, and reweight honestly.
