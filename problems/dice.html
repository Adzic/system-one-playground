The Even Dice Trap

You roll a fair six-sided die repeatedly until you roll a 6, and then you stop. Given that every single roll you observed, including the final 6, was an even number, what is the expected total number of rolls?

Beat 3 · The mechanism

The tempting answer is 3: every roll was even, so the die "effectively" had faces 2, 4, 6, and the expected wait for one face of three is 3. That reasoning is wrong, and it is worth seeing exactly where. Conditioning on all rolls being even does not rebuild the die. It reweights the possible histories, and it punishes long ones, because a long history has more chances to contain an odd roll and get disqualified.

Sort each roll into three kinds. Odd, probability 3/6. A 6, probability 1/6. Filler, meaning a 2 or a 4, probability 2/6. Filler rolls change nothing except the count. The condition holds exactly when the first decisive roll, the first roll that is odd or a 6, turns out to be the 6. And the number of filler rolls before that first decisive roll does not depend on which decisive face wins.

So count in two pieces. Filler arrives with probability 1/3 per roll against 2/3 for a decisive roll, so the expected number of fillers before the first decisive roll is (1/3)/(2/3) = 1/2. Add the final 6:

E[rolls | all even] = 1/2 + 1 = 3/2

Bayes agrees, as a check. The probability of stopping at roll n and satisfying the condition is (1/6)(1/3)n−1, since each pre-final roll must land on 2 or 4 out of the live possibilities. Summing over n, the condition itself has probability 1/4. Dividing through leaves a geometric distribution with ratio 1/3, whose mean is 3/2, and whose first term says two thirds of all conditioned runs end in exactly one roll. That is the simulation's "ended in 1 roll" number, and it is the whole intuition: most conditioned worlds are the boring one where you simply roll a 6 immediately.

Beat 4 · At work

Conditioning on what you observed is selection, and selection quietly reweights every average you compute afterward. Analyze only completed calls and you have conditioned on calls that did not get abandoned. Analyze only members who stayed enrolled and you have conditioned on survival. Analyze only trades, campaigns, or patients that made it into the dataset, and the dataset has already voted on which histories were allowed to be long. The dice version is harmless. The work version produces dashboards that describe the filtered world precisely, and the real world not at all. Whenever a number surprises you, the first question is not "is the math right" but "what did we condition on to get this sample."
