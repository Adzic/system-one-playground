PROBLEM 6 · CORRELATION BOUNDS

The Correlation That Cannot Exist

Three standardized variables. Your colleague sets \(\operatorname{Corr}(X_1, X_2) = 0.8\) and \(\operatorname{Corr}(X_2, X_3) = 0.8\), then, wanting \(X_1\) and \(X_3\) to be nearly unrelated, assumes \(\operatorname{Corr}(X_1, X_3) = 0.1\). It cannot be done. Not "it is unlikely": no three random variables anywhere satisfy all three numbers at once.

Beat 2 · See it

Standardized variables are unit vectors, and correlations are cosines of the angles between them. \(X_2\) points right. \(X_1\) sits at \(+36.87^\circ\), since \(\arccos 0.8 = 36.87^\circ\). \(X_3\) rides a cone around \(X_2\) at the same \(36.87^\circ\), so \(\operatorname{Corr}(X_2, X_3) = 0.8\) never changes; the drawing unrolls that ride into the plane. The slider swings \(X_3\) from sitting exactly on \(X_1\) to the far side of the cone. The only thing that moves is the angle between \(X_1\) and \(X_3\), and it cannot exceed \(36.87^\circ + 36.87^\circ = 73.74^\circ\).

Across the whole feasible band the determinant stays at or above zero and touches 0 at both ends, where the three vectors collapse into a plane figure with no volume. A negative determinant is the algebraic shadow of a geometric impossibility: three unit vectors cannot make the angles the numbers demand.

Beat 3 · The mechanism

A correlation matrix is a covariance matrix of standardized variables, so it must be positive semidefinite: \(v^\top R v = \operatorname{Var}(v^\top X) \ge 0\) for every \(v\), because a variance cannot be negative. For a \(3 \times 3\) matrix with unit diagonal, positive semidefiniteness reduces (given the pairwise entries are in \([-1, 1]\)) to one scalar condition: the determinant must be nonnegative. Write \(r\) for the unknown third correlation. Then

\[\det R = 1 + 2(0.8)(0.8)r - 0.8^2 - 0.8^2 - r^2 = -r^2 + 1.28r - 0.28 \ge 0\]

Solving the quadratic equality for its two roots,

\[r = \frac{1.28 \pm \sqrt{1.28^2 - 4(0.28)}}{2} = \frac{1.28 \pm 0.72}{2} \in [\,0.28,\ 1\,]\]

So the feasible interval is exactly \([0.28, 1]\), and \(0.1\) is not near the boundary, it is far outside it: forcing it gives \(\det R = -0.162\), a matrix with a negative eigenvalue, which would assign some portfolio of the three variables a negative variance. The general formula, worth memorizing, is that the third correlation is trapped within

\[\rho_{12}\rho_{23} \pm \sqrt{(1 - \rho_{12}^2)(1 - \rho_{23}^2)} = 0.64 \pm 0.36\]

The same interval falls out of a second door: the partial correlation \(\rho_{13 \cdot 2} = \dfrac{r - \rho_{12}\rho_{23}}{\sqrt{(1 - \rho_{12}^2)(1 - \rho_{23}^2)}}\) is a correlation like any other, so it must lie in \([-1, 1]\), and rearranging that single requirement reproduces the bound. Geometrically it is the picture above: correlations are cosines, the angle between \(X_1\) and \(X_3\) is at most the sum of the two pinned angles, \(73.74^\circ\), and \(\cos 73.74^\circ = 0.28\). Correlation is not transitive, \(0.8\) and \(0.8\) do not force \(0.64\) or anything like equality, but it is not free either. Two variables that each lean heavily on a third cannot be strangers to each other.

Beat 4 · At work

This trap is not a textbook ornament; it is a recurring production incident. Correlation matrices in risk models, scenario designs, and stress tests are routinely assembled by hand, one pairwise number at a time, each number plausible on its own. The assembled matrix is then fed to a simulator, which needs a Cholesky factor, which needs positive semidefiniteness. When the matrix is impossible, the failure is ugly in one of two ways: the factorization throws, the pipeline fills with NaNs, and someone has a bad afternoon; or, worse, a library quietly "fixes" things or the negative eigenvalue gets absorbed somewhere, and the simulation runs to completion emitting draws from a distribution that does not exist. The professional fixes are eigenvalue clipping and nearest-correlation-matrix methods, which project the hand-built matrix onto the nearest valid one, deliberately and with a paper trail. The lesson to internalize is the shape of the constraint: pairwise correlations are not independent dials. They are angles of a single rigid figure, and anyone who sets them one at a time will eventually build a matrix that cannot exist.
